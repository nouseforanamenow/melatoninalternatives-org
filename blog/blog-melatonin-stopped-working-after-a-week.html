Why Melatonin Stops Working After a Week

By Melatonin Alternatives Editorial Team | Published September 18, 2026 | 6 min read

When melatonin seems to fade after about a week, the usual assumption is tolerance, as if your body has grown numb to it. That is rarely the best explanation. More often the early lift was partly the boost of trying something new, and what faded was that first-week effect rather than the melatonin itself. Timing habits, evening light, and the nature of your sleep problem usually explain the rest.

The Direct Answer: Usually Not Classic Tolerance

Melatonin does not behave like the substances people associate with fast tolerance, where you need steadily more to feel the same thing. When it stops delivering after a week, the more common story is a mix of the early novelty wearing off and small mistakes in how it is being used. Reaching for a bigger dose answers a fade that may not be about dose at all.

Two different fades Tolerance is your body responding less to the same amount over time. A placebo fade is the early lift of a new routine settling back toward your true baseline. They feel identical from the inside, but they call for different responses.

Tolerance vs the Placebo Fade

The first few nights on any new sleep aid often feel better than the nights that follow. Part of that is genuine and part of it is expectation, optimism, and the simple attention you are paying to your sleep. As the novelty settles, your experience drifts back toward your real baseline. Nothing about the melatonin changed. The extra lift you felt at the start was never going to hold at that height.

People read that settling as the pill wearing off, then conclude they have built tolerance and need more. In reality they may be watching a placebo effect fade on schedule. The tell is that a larger dose often produces another short-lived bump followed by the same fade, which is the pattern of expectation rather than a dose problem.

Timing and Light Mistakes That Look Like Failure

Before blaming the molecule, it is worth checking the two levers most people get wrong.

  • Taking it too late. Your own evening melatonin rises a couple of hours before sleep. A supplement taken at the very moment you want to be asleep can arrive too late to reinforce that timing well.
  • Bright light after the dose. Scrolling a bright phone or sitting under strong light after taking melatonin works directly against the night-time signal you just tried to send. Light is a powerful cue, and it can override a modest dose.
  • An inconsistent schedule. Wildly different bed and wake times keep your internal clock unsettled, so no amount of melatonin lands the same way two nights running.
  • A dose that overshoots. A large amount can linger into the morning and leave grogginess that gets mistaken for the pill no longer working at night.

Fixing these often revives the effect that people assumed was gone for good.

When the Problem Is Not Melatonin-Shaped

Here is the harder possibility. Melatonin is a timing signal. It tells your body when night is, and it does that job reasonably well for circadian issues such as jet lag, shift work, or a schedule that has drifted late.

It does not calm a racing mind, quiet anxiety, or settle a nervous system stuck in high gear. If your nights are defined by worry, rumination, or feeling wired but tired, melatonin was addressing the wrong layer from the start. In that case it did not stop working after a week. It was never the right match, and the first-week lift simply masked that for a while.

This is why so many people cycle through larger and larger melatonin doses without lasting relief. They are asking a clock to solve a calm problem, and no dose of a timing signal turns into a calming agent.

What to Reassess

Rather than sizing up, run through a short reassessment.

  1. Name the real problem. Is it timing, meaning you cannot fall asleep until very late, or is it a mind that will not settle? The answer points to entirely different supports.
  2. Fix timing and light first. Take any melatonin earlier, dim your evenings, and hold a steady wake time for a couple of weeks before drawing conclusions.
  3. Try smaller, not larger. If grogginess is part of the picture, a smaller amount taken earlier often works better than a bigger one.
  4. Match the tool to the problem. For a busy mind, people look to calm-focused options rather than the clock, since those address a different layer than melatonin does.

Who Should Be Cautious

If sleep trouble has lasted for weeks, keeps returning, or comes with daytime distress, that is a reason to speak with a clinician rather than to keep experimenting alone. The same is true if you take other medications, live with an ongoing health condition, are pregnant or nursing, or are considering melatonin for a child or teen. A professional can help sort a timing problem from something that deserves a closer look.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you build a tolerance to melatonin?

Classic tolerance, where you need steadily more for the same effect, is not the typical explanation for melatonin fading. More often the early lift of a new routine settles back toward your baseline, which feels like tolerance but responds differently. A larger dose usually just repeats that short-lived pattern.

Why did melatonin work at first and then stop?

The first nights on anything new often feel better because of novelty and expectation on top of any real effect. As that settles, you return to your true baseline. Timing errors, evening light, and an inconsistent schedule can also erode the effect you felt at the start.

Should I increase my dose if it stopped working?

Increasing is the common reflex and often the wrong one. If the fade is a placebo settling or a timing and light mistake, a bigger dose mainly adds morning grogginess. Fixing timing, dimming your evenings, and matching the tool to your actual problem tend to help more.

What if none of this helps?

If your sleep problem is really about a mind that will not settle, melatonin was never the right match, and no dose changes that. Persistent or distressing sleep trouble is worth discussing with a clinician who can look at the underlying cause.

A fade after a week is usually a clue, not a dead end.
